# Mission and governance pivot

**Status:** SKETCH
**Date:** 2026-07-19
**Relates to:** [`current/02-entity-structure.md`](../current/02-entity-structure.md) (PROPOSED — this draft, if adopted, would revise it), the unwritten `current/01-mission-and-scope.md` and `current/03-governance.md`

This draft captures a change in direction discussed in July 2026. If adopted, it needs a decision record and updates to `current/02-entity-structure.md` and the website. Read with skepticism.

## Summary of the pivot

Three connected moves:

1. **Broaden the mission beyond the transaction.** The Trust's mission is neighborhood stewardship, not the acquisition of one property. If we never buy real estate, the Trust still has work to do.
2. **Self-perpetuating board instead of resident-member elections.** No membership rolls, no residency verification, no elections. The board selects its own successors.
3. **Simplify the investment structure.** Step back from the multi-class securities design (Class A/B/V/C, Reg D 506(c) + Reg CF/A+ + intrastate). If an investment vehicle is needed, it's a single simple LLC tied to the Trust: people contribute capital and are granted shares in accordance with a valuation.

## Proposed mission statement

> The Downtown Loyal Heights Trust stewards the Loyal Heights neighborhood of Seattle, ensuring that growth is shaped by, governed by, and benefits the people who live, work, and gather there — for generations.

### Why this wording

**Not tied to real estate.** Earlier drafts framed the mission around community-owned redevelopment of 80th & 24th. A mission tied to one transaction dies with the transaction. Under this mission, if the Trust can't buy real estate it can still convene, educate, publish the replicable model, advocate in planning processes, hold covenants or easements, or partner with whoever does buy.

**"The people who live, work, and gather there."** Deliberately broader than "residents." It includes the people who come to Loyal Heights for work, school, medical care, shopping, and everything else — and it includes neighbors in adjacent small neighborhoods, especially Sunset Hill, whose participation we actively want. It also means the founding documents never have to define where Loyal Heights ends. (The official city boundary is too narrow; the site's FAQ already says so. This wording lets the boundary stay a fun board discussion instead of a legal definition.)

**Broad benefit class helps the 501(c)(3) case.** Serving everyone who uses the neighborhood looks more like a public class than serving a defined set of resident-investors. The formal purposes clause in the articles will still need charitable framing from counsel (e.g., "to promote social welfare and combat deterioration of the Loyal Heights neighborhood of Seattle"); the mission statement rides on top and need not be the same sentence.

## Governance: self-perpetuating board

Instead of the earlier sketch — Trust members are resident shareholders who elect the board — the board is self-selecting:

- The board fills its own vacancies and selects successors.
- Expectation: mostly Loyal Heights residents, but the board has discretion to seat non-residents who are a good fit (adjacent-neighborhood participants, subject-matter experts, anchor-scale supporters).
- No membership rolls, no residency verification, no contested elections to administer.

This is the default structure for Washington nonprofit corporations under RCW 24.03A (a "no members" nonprofit) and by far the simpler path.

**Trade-off to be honest about:** the site's current governance sketch promises democratic accountability ("accountable to the neighborhood rather than an English duke"). A self-perpetuating board rests its legitimacy on conduct and composition rather than elections. Plenty of respected community organizations work this way, but the website copy should be updated so the promise matches the structure. Options for keeping the accountability flavor without formal membership: public board meetings, published minutes, an advisory council open to anyone, bylaws requiring a supermajority of board seats to be held by residents of the greater neighborhood.

## Investment structure: keep it simple

The earlier design (three or four classes of LLC interests, phased Reg D / Reg CF / Reg A+ / intrastate offerings) is set aside as premature. The working idea now:

- The Trust itself never sells securities or holds investor capital. (Unchanged.)
- If and when there is something to invest in, form **one LLC**, tied to the Trust (likely: Trust as manager, or Trust holding a special interest that carries the mission lock — for counsel).
- Contributions to the LLC are simple: a contributor puts in capital and receives units in accordance with a then-current valuation. One class, pro-rata, no phased offering architecture.
- Securities law still applies to even a simple LLC raising money from neighbors — counsel must bless the exemption before anything moves. But the surface area is much smaller than the previous design.

The "anyone can invest, only residents vote" principle from the site is softened by this pivot. If it survives, it lives in the LLC agreement or in the Trust's reserved powers, not in a dedicated voting-share class.

## Open questions for counsel

1. Formal purposes clause for the articles that supports 501(c)(3) recognition while the public mission statement stays as written above.
2. Correct mechanism to "tie" the LLC to the Trust (manager interest vs. special member interest vs. contractual mission lock) given the Trust's exempt status.
3. Whether a nonprofit with a self-perpetuating board managing a for-profit LLC raises unrelated-business-income or private-benefit issues, and how the affordable/community elements should be positioned in the 1023.
4. Which securities exemption fits a single-class LLC raising from a mix of accredited and non-accredited neighbors.
